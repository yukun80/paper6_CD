\title{Where Is the Flood, and How Deep? DDFlood for SAR-Based Flood Mapping}

\author{}

\begin{abstract}

Rapid flood assessment requires information on both the spatial distribution and depth of inundation. Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) change detection identifies changes in inundation between pre-event and post-event observations, while boundary-elevation-constrained methods combined with a digital elevation model (DEM) estimate water depth from flood extent and terrain. Connecting these methods requires a common spatial support. Flood-induced backscatter changes vary in strength, inundation takes diverse spatial forms, and elevations along detected boundaries vary locally, making it difficult for a single boundary water level to translate detection results into stable conditions for depth estimation. We therefore propose DDFlood, a two-stage flood mapping framework that uses the newly inundated extent produced by HarmoSSM as the spatial support for Change-Constrained Flood Depth Estimation (CCDepth). HarmoSSM combines bi-temporal state and difference information through Harmonized Alignment (HA) and Change Query Interaction (CQI), and reconstructs inundation extent by combining regional context and local detail through an Omni-Scale State-Space Decoder (OSSD). CCDepth constructs water-level intervals from terrain statistics on both sides of the boundary, reconstructs the water surface under terrain lower-bound and continuity constraints, and calculates water depth. Bi-temporal GF3 and LT-1B SAR imagery is used to evaluate detection performance for three events characterized by scattered inundation in Zhengzhou, contiguous inundation in Zhuozhou, and fragmented flooded areas in Guangxi. ICESat-2 along-track reference depths from the 2022 Pakistan floods are used to validate depth estimates. HarmoSSM achieves intersection over union (IoU) values of 79.45\%, 77.83\%, and 81.79\% in Zhengzhou, Zhuozhou, and Guangxi, respectively, exceeding all eight comparison methods, with recall above 80\% in all three areas. In the Pakistan validation area, CCDepth achieves a mean absolute error of 0.4910~m and a root mean square error of 0.6917~m, reductions of 43.8\% and 35.2\% relative to FwDET v2.0, respectively. Positive-depth coverage is 92.36\% for CCDepth and 55.96\% for FwDET, with median water-surface elevation gradients of 3.73‰ and 28.87‰, respectively. When the manually delineated reference extent and the detected extent are used as alternative inputs, the depth-class agreement proportions of CCDepth range from 0.66 to 0.82 in the three study areas. CCDepth requires less depth-estimation time than FwDET in all four evaluation areas. DDFlood connects newly inundated extent identification with terrain-constrained depth estimation to provide flood extent and depth information for rapid flood assessment.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Floods are among the world's most frequent and widespread natural disasters, posing serious threats to human life and socioeconomic activity \citep{credUndrr2020disasters}. Satellite observations indicate that the proportion of the population exposed within observed inundated areas is increasing \citep{tellman2021exposure}. Timely information on flood extent and water depth is essential for emergency response and post-disaster assessment. Synthetic aperture radar (SAR) uses active microwave imaging to acquire surface information under cloudy and rainy conditions and at night, and has become an important data source for operational flood monitoring \citep{wagner2026gfm}.

Inundation extent and water depth provide complementary flood information. Extent identifies affected areas, while depth describes the degree of submergence within them. Together, they support risk assessments for people and assets. Assessments of risks to people and vehicles during the Zhengzhou floods, for example, combined inundation conditions with hydraulic instability conditions \citep{dong2022urbanrisk}. These two types of information are obtained on different bases. Specular reflection from smooth open water generally reduces backscatter, providing a basis for identifying inundation extent \citep{amitrano2024sarFloodReview}. Depth estimation additionally incorporates terrain information. Terrain elevations at inundation boundaries serve as water-level references for constructing a water-surface elevation field within the extent, and depth is then calculated as the difference between water-surface and ground elevations \citep{cian2018flood}. In this process, the identified extent determines both the spatial support and the boundary references for depth estimation.

Extensive research has been conducted on SAR-based flood water identification and post-flood water extent mapping \citep{jiang2021rapid,lee2026smagnet}. Threshold segmentation is suited to areas with homogeneous land cover and strong contrast between water and land, and deep learning improves water identification in complex settings through multiscale features and context modeling \citep{du2026high}. Bi-temporal change detection compares pre-event and post-event observations to provide temporal information for identifying newly inundated extents \citep{giustarini2013urbanFloodCd}. Open water typically exhibits low backscatter, while flooding beneath vegetation may increase backscatter through multiple scattering \citep{tsyganskaya2018vegetation}. These scattering differences produce flood-induced changes of varying strength \citep{amitrano2024sarFloodReview}. Weak changes in scattered patches, narrow strips, and vegetated areas are easily missed, whereas arid sandy surfaces and building shadows may exhibit low backscatter similar to water and be confused with inundation. Layover in built-up areas mixes scattering responses and further complicates inundation identification \citep{garg2024aridFlood,yu2024urbanFloodThreshold}. Identifying newly inundated extents therefore requires consideration of both surface states and change responses at the two times.

Existing feature fusion and context modeling methods improve change separability \citep{saleh2024dam,du2026high}, while bi-temporal feature comparison also requires attention to statistical comparability and local positional correspondence. Differences in imaging conditions, speckle, and local positions cause difference responses to contain both observation differences and surface changes. Calibration with shared bi-temporal statistics provides a common comparison basis, and deformable sampling \citep{zhu2021deformable} allows local positional correspondence to be adjusted. Statistical calibration reduces the influence of observation differences on change responses, and local alignment reduces apparent changes caused by misregistration. Jointly encoding pre-event states, post-event states, signed differences, and absolute differences combines surface appearance with the direction and magnitude of change. Harmonizing bi-temporal features and jointly organizing state and difference information are therefore important aspects of flood-change encoding.

Flood-extent reconstruction must preserve the regional completeness of contiguous inundation and the local shapes of narrow flooded strips and scattered standing water. Multiscale representations express scale differences by fusing features at different levels or introducing feature pyramids \citep{wu2022msdeeplab,zhao2023siamdwenet,tahermanesh2025siscnet}, while feature-selection methods organize multiscale information through attention weighting or adaptive windows \citep{yadav2022attentive,zhu2025globalfloodsar}. Bi-temporal semantic interaction and deep change priors further provide regional context for feature fusion \citep{chen2021remote,han2023change}. Contiguous regions require context over larger areas, whereas narrow strips and scattered patches require high-resolution edge and positional information. Regional aggregation may smooth local variations, whereas detail preservation alone may fragment contiguous regions. Mamba and VMamba model long-range dependencies with computational complexity that scales linearly with sequence length \citep{gu2023mamba,liu2024vmamba}, and ChangeMamba applies state-space modeling to bi-temporal feature interaction in remote sensing \citep{chen2024changemamba}. These studies provide a basis for regional context modeling, and flood-extent reconstruction requires a decoder that combines deep regional aggregation with shallow detail recovery.

Water-depth estimation methods mainly comprise hydrodynamic models, data-driven models, and boundary-elevation-constrained methods. Hydrodynamic models simulate flood evolution by solving the Saint-Venant equations or simplified hydraulic relationships and require detailed channel topography and roughness parameters \citep{teng2017floodInundationUncertainty}. Data-driven models use machine learning to regress water depth from SAR imagery and auxiliary data, relying on measured depth samples for training \citep{chu2025urbanDepth}. Boundary-elevation-constrained methods take flood extent and a DEM as inputs and are suited to rapid disaster assessment. Cian et al. estimated water levels from lidar terrain elevations at inundation margins \citep{cian2018flood}. FwDET derives water depth from inundation extent and terrain \citep{cohen2018estimating}. Version 2.0 improves boundary identification and assigns water levels to interior pixels using the elevations of the nearest boundary pixels \citep{cohen2019floodwater}. FLEXTH constrains water-level propagation using terrain conditions \citep{betterle2024water}. RS-FloodXDepth and Yang et al. connect water-level references with hydrologically guided region growing and water segmentation, respectively \citep{tian2026floodxdepth,yang2026csmamba}. Constructing and propagating boundary elevation references are common elements of these methods. Cohen et al.'s analysis of FwDET v2.1 shows that depth estimates are relatively sensitive to boundary smoothing and filtering parameters when medium- and coarse-resolution DEMs are used \citep{cohen2022sensitivity}.

The input extent affects depth estimation through the locations and number of boundary samples and their connectivity structure. In their comparison of flood-processing chains, Travert et al. found that input flood maps and method hyperparameters were important factors affecting depth results \citep{travert2026uncertainty}. When the input comes from change detection, the boundary of the newly inundated extent may adjoin pre-existing water bodies, unchanged higher terrain, or detection errors, so boundary samples mix elevation references with different meanings, and the connectivity structure may differ from that of the actual water body. Omissions and false alarms in change-detection masks alter boundary positions, and the reliability of boundary elevation references is an important condition for these depth-estimation methods. When a single boundary elevation serves as the reference, local elevation errors can affect interior estimates through water-level assignment. Expressing boundary references as intervals and solving jointly with terrain lower bounds and within-component water-surface continuity helps use both boundary and interior terrain information. Change-detection research focuses on identifying and spatially reconstructing newly inundated areas, whereas boundary-elevation-based depth research focuses on water-level references and propagation within a given flood extent. Connecting these tasks requires an explicit common solution domain so that detection results directly determine the spatial support and boundary references for depth estimation, with input-extent comparison used to evaluate their influence on depth results.

To address these needs, we propose Detection-to-Depth Flood Mapping (DDFlood), a two-stage framework that combines bi-temporal SAR imagery with a DEM to map newly inundated extent and water depth within a shared spatial support. HarmoSSM generates the newly inundated extent in the detection stage. In the depth-estimation stage, Change-Constrained Flood Depth Estimation (CCDepth) combines the detected extent with a DEM to reconstruct the water surface and output water depth. The main contributions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Harmonized encoding and omni-scale reconstruction for flood change detection.} We design HarmoSSM with a Harmonized Change Encoder (HCE) and an Omni-Scale State-Space Decoder (OSSD). Within HCE, Harmonized Alignment (HA) calibrates features using shared bi-temporal statistics and adjusts local positional correspondence, while Change Query Interaction (CQI) jointly encodes pre-event states, post-event states, signed differences, and absolute differences. HCE provides comparable bi-temporal change representations for identifying weak changes. OSSD aggregates regional context in deep change features and recovers local detail in shallow features, combining both types of information to reconstruct newly inundated extents of different morphologies.

  \item \textbf{Depth estimation constrained by the change support domain.} We construct CCDepth using the median and median absolute deviation of terrain elevations on both sides of the boundary to form water-level intervals, with inner-side terrain statistics providing the lower reference and outer-side terrain statistics providing the upper reference. Robust statistics and boundary weights reduce the influence of local fluctuations in boundary elevation on the water-surface solution. Soft interval constraints in the boundary band, hard interior terrain lower bounds, and a weighted sum of squared water-surface elevation differences between adjacent pixels jointly define the water-surface optimization problem. Water-surface elevations are solved jointly within connected components with sufficient boundary support, and water depth is then calculated pixelwise.

  \item \textbf{Two-stage mapping with shared spatial support and input-extent comparison.} DDFlood uses the HarmoSSM-detected extent and the DEM together to determine the CCDepth solution domain and boundary references. Water surfaces are solved independently within eight-connected components, so detection errors affect depth through the solution domain, boundary samples, and connectivity structure of the components in which they occur. With the DEM, solver parameters, and depth-class thresholds fixed, an input-extent comparison between manually delineated reference extents and detected extents is conducted to analyze how detection errors propagate into depth classes and output coverage. The framework is evaluated on GF3 and LT-1B imagery from three flood events, and the depth estimates of CCDepth are validated with ICESat-2 reference depths from the 2022 Pakistan floods.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the study areas and data. Section 3 presents the DDFlood framework and the designs of HarmoSSM and CCDepth. Section 4 describes the experimental setup. Section 5 reports the results of change detection, depth estimation, and framework performance. Section 6 discusses the depth-constraint mechanisms, detection-error propagation, and limitations. Section 7 concludes the paper.

\section{Study Areas and Data}

\subsection{Flood Events and Study Areas}

Three study areas are selected for change detection and change-constrained depth estimation, and a separate flood event is selected for independent depth validation. The four events differ in flood causes, inundated objects, and surface conditions (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}).

From 17 to 22 July 2021, persistent heavy rainfall affected Henan Province, causing severe urban inundation in central Zhengzhou. Roads and low-lying areas were flooded, affecting vehicle safety \citep{dong2022urbanrisk}. The Zhengzhou study area covers built-up land interspersed with channels, roads, and low-lying terrain. Newly inundated areas mainly comprise scattered patches and locally narrow strips, providing a setting for examining the identification of scattered standing water, complex boundaries, and weak change responses in a built environment.

From late July to early August 2023, the remnants of Typhoon Doksuri caused an extreme basin-wide flood in the Haihe basin. Parts of the cropland and residential areas in Zhuozhou, Hebei Province, experienced contiguous inundation, with inundation boundaries difficult to distinguish from pre-existing water bodies. The Zhuozhou study area mainly comprises interspersed cropland and settlements. Its reference inundation is relatively concentrated and contiguous, allowing examination of spatial continuity and local boundaries across different surface types.

In early July 2026, Typhoon Maysak brought persistent heavy rainfall to Nanning. Liuwang Reservoir in Binyang County overflowed its dam, while Liulan and Yunbiao reservoirs in Hengzhou experienced overtopping and breaches, inundating some downstream villages, towns, and cropland. The Guangxi study area is located near Binyang County and Hengzhou and is defined by the common valid coverage of the bi-temporal LT-1B imagery. Forest, cropland, and settlements form a mosaic, and inundation comprises multiple disconnected areas, irregular boundaries, and scattered fragments. This area is used to examine detection adaptability in a setting with multiple types of change.

From July to September 2022, Pakistan experienced persistent heavy monsoon rainfall and extensive flooding in the Indus basin, affecting approximately 33 million people, causing more than 1,700 deaths, and resulting in approximately EUR 20 billion in direct losses \citep{nanditha2023pakistan,betterle2024water}. The Pakistan validation area lies in the middle and lower Indus Plain, mainly in Sindh Province and extending into eastern Balochistan and southern Punjab (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}(e)). Flooding was concentrated around Larkana, Shikarpur, and Jacobabad in northern Sindh \citep{betterle2024water}. Approximately 2.5 million hm\textsuperscript{2}, or 18\% of Sindh's area, was inundated, including approximately 1.1 million hm\textsuperscript{2} of cropland \citep{qamer2023cropLosses}. The area is low-lying and flat, dominated by irrigated cropland, and experienced extensive contiguous inundation that persisted for a prolonged period.

The data are divided into three groups by purpose. SAR imagery and change-detection samples support model training and scene testing, terrain data provide ground elevations for depth estimation, and validation and reference data support depth evaluation and local site comparisons.

Bi-temporal SAR imagery is used for the three detection study areas: GF3 HV-polarized imagery for Zhengzhou and Zhuozhou, and LT-1B HH-polarized imagery for Guangxi. The image pairs in all three areas have undergone radiometric tone harmonization and geometric registration and have been standardized to a pixel spacing of approximately 10 m. S1GFloods and VarFloods cover different flood events and surface conditions, while a small number of Zhengzhou GF3 images supplement local GF3 observation conditions. FABDEM provides terrain data for the three detection study areas and the Pakistan validation area. It is derived from the Copernicus GLO-30 DEM by using machine learning to remove building and forest height biases, with a spatial resolution of 1 arc-second (approximately 30 m near the equator). The data used here were obtained through the Google Earth Engine Community Catalog \citep{hawker2022fabdem,fabdemGEE}.

The Pakistan validation data comprise flood extent and along-track reference depths. Flood extent is derived from scene-level Global Flood Monitoring (GFM) products, which provide three layers: observed flood extent (F), a reference water mask (R), and an exclusion mask (X). Flood pixels are selected where all three layers are valid and \(F=1\), \(R=0\), and \(X=0\); all other pixels are assigned NoData \citep{gfm2025pum}. Scene-level results from July to September 2022 are aggregated over time to obtain the validation-area flood extent and cover the main inundation process during the monsoon period \citep{betterle2024water}. Along-track reference depths are constructed from wet-period minus dry-period elevations of medium- and high-confidence strong-beam photons from ICESat-2 ATL03. The wet-period observations were acquired on 3 September 2022, and the spatially matched dry-period track was acquired on 6 June 2021; the two tracks are separated by approximately 50 m across-track. Photon elevations are smoothed with a moving median filter using a 100-photon window, and along-track candidate points are then generated at latitude increments corresponding to approximately 5 m. Dry-period elevations represent ground elevation, and wet-period minus dry-period elevations define reference depth, retaining both positive and negative differences. The final set contains 14,902 along-track reference points with depths greater than 0.01 m.

Local site comparison data comprise social-media flood photographs S1--S12 and their depth intervals, together with literature depth records LS1--LS3 \citep{zhang2024yolomaskrcnn}. Photograph annotations identify inundated objects and their reference depth intervals, while the literature records provide observed depths at local sites. Both data sources are used to compare depth estimates for the corresponding connected components.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Overall Framework}\label{sec:overview}

Flood mapping needs to describe both where new inundation occurs and the degree of submergence. Change detection locates changes in water extent, while depth estimation uses terrain elevations to establish the relationship between the water surface and ground within a given extent. DDFlood organizes these tasks into a sequential mapping workflow. The newly inundated mask produced by HarmoSSM directly determines the valid computational extent for CCDepth, so depth results share the spatial support of the detected extent.

The framework connects extent identification and depth estimation through a shared spatial domain. Pre-event and post-event SAR images produce a newly inundated mask, the DEM provides ground elevations, and the detection mask and valid terrain area jointly delimit the depth computational domain. The CCDepth solution support is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:framework_support}
 \Omega_f=\{p:\hat{\mathbf M}(p)=1\}\cap\Omega_Z,\qquad
 \hat{\mathbf M}=\Phi_{\mathrm H}(\mathbf I^A,\mathbf I^B),\qquad
 \hat d=\Phi_{\mathrm C}(\hat{\mathbf M},Z),
\end{equation}
where \(\Phi_{\mathrm H}\) denotes the HarmoSSM change-detection mapping, \(\Phi_{\mathrm C}\) denotes the CCDepth depth-estimation mapping, \(\Omega_f\) delimits the valid depth-estimation extent, and \(\hat d\) is estimated water depth. HarmoSSM outputs the newly inundated mask \(\hat{\mathbf M}\) and foreground probability \(p_f\). The mask and decision threshold determine \(\Omega_f\). CCDepth solves for the water surface within \(\Omega_f\) and outputs a depth map \(\hat d\) and a component state map \(\mathrm{status}\), assigning NoData to pixels that do not meet the solution conditions. The detected extent defines where depth is calculated, while terrain and boundary intervals define the conditions for solving the water surface. These outputs form the unified mapping workflow in Fig.~\ref{fig:framework}.

\subsection{HarmoSSM for Flood Change Detection}\label{sec:harmossm}

HarmoSSM performs bi-temporal SAR flood change detection using HCE and OSSD. HCE establishes shared bi-temporal features and generates five levels of change features through HA and CQI. OSSD builds regional context from deep features and then recovers flood extent using shallow features. The two components organize change features and perform spatial reconstruction, respectively.

\subsubsection{Harmonized Change Encoder}\label{sec:encoder}\label{sec:ha}\label{sec:cqi}

HCE constructs structurally consistent multiscale features for pre-event and post-event images. A shared EfficientNet-B2 and FPN generate a feature pyramid from \(P1\) to \(P5\) \citep{tan2019efficientnet,lin2017fpn}. Shallow features retain position and texture information, whereas deep features represent surface structures over larger areas. Both times use the same CNN encoding weights; \(\mathbf P_i^t\) denotes the feature at level \(i\) and time \(t\in\{A,B\}\).

Features from layers 5, 8, and 11 of a frozen DINOv3 ViT-S/16 are adapted within each time branch and fused into \(P3\) to \(P5\) \citep{simeoni2025dinov3}. The fusion branch applies convolution, normalization, and CBAM in sequence \citep{woo2018cbam}, providing stable semantic context for deep convolutional features. The CNN and DINO branches each share weights between the two times, while semantic fusion is performed independently within each time branch. HCE passes \(P1\) to \(P5\) through HA and then CQI to produce five levels of change features, \(\mathbf D_1\) to \(\mathbf D_5\).

\paragraph{Harmonized Alignment (HA)}

HA establishes comparable bi-temporal features at \(P1\) to \(P3\). SAR image pairs differ in imaging statistics, speckle, and local positional correspondence, so direct comparison mixes appearance differences with actual change. At these levels, HA applies statistical calibration and local deformable alignment to bring pre-event and post-event features into a common feature space and provide spatially consistent inputs for subsequent change interaction. Inputs at level \(i\) are denoted by \(\mathbf P_i^A\) and \(\mathbf P_i^B\), and both times contribute to the channel means and standard deviations:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ha_calibration}
 \boldsymbol\mu_i^{AB}=\operatorname{mean}_{t,\mathbf p}\mathbf P_i^t(\mathbf p),\qquad
 \sigma_i^{AB}=\sqrt{\operatorname{var}_{t,\mathbf p}\mathbf P_i^t(\mathbf p)+\epsilon},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ha_affine}
 \mathbf Y_i^t=(1/\sigma_i^{AB})(\mathbf P_i^t-\boldsymbol\mu_i^{AB})\odot
 [\operatorname{softplus}(\mathbf s_i^c)+\epsilon]+\boldsymbol\mu_i^c,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ha_recovery}
 \mathbf U_i^t=\mathbf Y_i^t+\mathcal{R}_i(\mathbf Y_i^t),
 \qquad i\in\{1,2,3\},
\end{equation}
where \(\epsilon\) is a numerical stability term, \(\boldsymbol\mu_i^c\) and \(\mathbf s_i^c\) are learned affine parameters shared between the two times, and \(\mathcal R_i\) consists of a \(3\times3\) convolution, batch normalization, SiLU, a \(1\times1\) convolution, and batch normalization. Both times use the same statistics and affine parameters, placing the calibrated \(\mathbf U_i^A\) and \(\mathbf U_i^B\) in a common feature space. \(\mathcal R_i\) preserves shallow texture and compensates for information lost during calibration.

After statistical calibration, HA performs local deformable alignment at \(P1\) to \(P3\) \citep{zhu2021deformable}. The calibrated post-event features act as queries and the pre-event features as keys and values. Local offsets and attention biases are predicted from \(\mathbf U_i^A\), \(\mathbf U_i^B\), and \(\mathbf U_i^B-\mathbf U_i^A\). Sampling locations and attention weights are written as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ha_alignment}
 \mathbf p'_{ik}(\mathbf p)=\mathbf p+\mathbf r_k+\Delta\mathbf p_{ik}(\mathbf p),
 \qquad
 \mathbf K_{s,i}(\mathbf p)=\mathbf K_i(\mathbf p'_{ik}),
 \qquad
 \mathbf V_{s,i}(\mathbf p)=\mathbf V_i(\mathbf p'_{ik}),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ha_output}
 a_{ik}(\mathbf p)=\operatorname{softmax}_k\!\left(
 \frac{\mathbf q_i(\mathbf p)^{\top}\mathbf K_{s,i}(\mathbf p)}{\sqrt d}
 +b_{ik}(\mathbf p)\right),\quad
 \mathbf X_i^A=\mathbf U_i^A+\mathcal O_i\!\left(
 \sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{ik}\mathbf V_{s,i}\right),\quad
 \mathbf X_i^B=\mathbf U_i^B.
\end{equation}
where \(\mathbf p\) is a grid location, \(\mathbf r_k\) is a fixed reference offset, \(\Delta\mathbf p_{ik}\) is a learned offset, \(b_{ik}\) is an attention bias, and \(K\) is the number of sampling points. A grouped convolutional branch predicts offsets and biases from \(\mathbf U_i^A\), \(\mathbf U_i^B\), and \(\mathbf U_i^B-\mathbf U_i^A\). \(\mathbf q_i\) is projected from \(\mathbf U_i^B\), while \(\mathbf K_i\) and \(\mathbf V_i\) are projected from \(\mathbf U_i^A\). HA alignment is applied at \(P1\) to \(P3\); \(P4\) and \(P5\) retain the encoder's semantically fused features. HA outputs the aligned bi-temporal features \(\mathbf X_i^A\) and \(\mathbf X_i^B\).

\paragraph{Change Query Interaction (CQI)}

CQI receives \(\mathbf X_i^A\) and \(\mathbf X_i^B\) from HA and combines pre-event states, post-event states, signed differences, and absolute differences at the same scale. Together, these four types of information describe the states and change direction of the bi-temporal features. The paired feature at level \(i\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cqi_pair}
  \mathbf{Z}_{i}
  =
  \mathcal{P}_{i}\!\left(
  \left[
  \mathbf{X}_{i}^{A},
  \mathbf{X}_{i}^{B},
  \mathbf{X}_{i}^{B}-\mathbf{X}_{i}^{A},
  \left|\mathbf{X}_{i}^{B}-\mathbf{X}_{i}^{A}\right|
  \right]\right),
  \quad i=1,\ldots,5,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal P_i\) is a convolutional pairing projection, brackets denote channel concatenation, and \(\mathbf Z_i\) retains all four types of change cues at each spatial location. \(\mathbf Z_i\) is flattened into spatial tokens \(\mathbf T_i\), which provide the dense input for bidirectional attention.

Each scale uses its own learnable queries \(\mathbf Q_i^0\). CQI first lets the queries aggregate global information from \(\mathbf T_i\), then passes the updated query information back to the tokens. Both updates use residual forms:
\begin{align}
 \mathbf{Q}_{i}^{1}
 &=\operatorname{LN}_{q,i}\!\left[\mathbf{Q}_{i}^{0}
 +\operatorname{MHA}_{q\leftarrow t}(\mathbf{Q}_{i}^{0},\mathbf{T}_{i},\mathbf{T}_{i})\right],\label{eq:cqi_query}\\
 \mathbf{T}_{i}^{1}
 &=\operatorname{LN}_{t,i}\!\left[\mathbf{T}_{i}
 +\operatorname{MHA}_{t\leftarrow q}(\mathbf{T}_{i},\mathbf{Q}_{i}^{1},\mathbf{Q}_{i}^{1})\right].\label{eq:cqi_token}
\end{align}
where MHA denotes multi-head attention and \(\operatorname{LN}\) denotes layer normalization. The second update uses the original tokens as queries, so the returned information corresponds to the original grid locations. The updated tokens pass through a feed-forward network (FFN) and are restored to spatial features:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cqi_output}
 \mathbf{D}_i=\mathcal{O}_i\!\left[
 \operatorname{Reshape}\!\left(\operatorname{LN}_{t,i}\left[\mathbf{T}_{i}^{1}+\operatorname{FFN}_i(\mathbf{T}_{i}^{1})\right]\right)+\mathbf{Z}_i\right].
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal O_i\) is an output mapping comprising a depthwise separable convolution and CBAM. The residual term \(\mathbf Z_i\) retains the original paired information, while \(\mathcal O_i\) adjusts channel and spatial responses. The five scales independently produce \(\mathbf D_1\) to \(\mathbf D_5\), which are passed to OSSD.

\subsubsection{Omni-Scale State-Space Decoder}\label{sec:oscd}

OSSD reconstructs the five levels of CQI change features into dense flood predictions through deep context modeling and progressive shallow-feature recovery (Fig.~\ref{fig:oscd}). The decoder builds regional context from \(\mathbf D_3\) to \(\mathbf D_5\), then progressively recovers local positions using \(\mathbf D_2\) and \(\mathbf D_1\), before a classification head produces pixelwise predictions. Deep features are projected onto the level-3 grid and fused into \(\mathbf F\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oscd_deep_fusion}
 \mathbf E_i=\operatorname{Resize}_{3}\!\left(\rho_i^c(\mathbf D_i)\right),
 \quad i\in\{3,4,5\},\qquad
 \mathbf F=\operatorname{Fuse}\!\left[\mathbf E_3,\mathbf E_4,\mathbf E_5\right].
\end{equation}
where \(\rho_i^c\) is a channel projection, \(\operatorname{Resize}_3\) resizes features to level 3, and \(\operatorname{Fuse}\) performs convolutional fusion after concatenation. \(\mathbf E_3\) to \(\mathbf E_5\) are retained for subsequent regional reconstruction so that the regional information obtained by scanning can be returned to each deep feature.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=.95\linewidth,keepaspectratio]{figure/Figure5.jpg}
  \caption{Structure of the Omni-Scale State-Space Decoder (OSSD). The left panel shows the deep context modeling and progressive shallow-feature recovery paths; the upper right shows regional context construction, and the lower right shows multiresolution input construction.}
  \label{fig:oscd}
\end{figure}

Regional context is constructed using multiresolution inputs and state-space scanning. \(\mathbf F\) is processed along paths at its original scale and with downsampling factors of two and four. The first two paths are rearranged onto a common grid by pixel unshuffle, followed by channel compression and concatenation to form the scanning input:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oscd_scan_input}
 \mathbf G=\left[
 \rho_0\!\left(\operatorname{PU}_{4}(\mathbf F)\right),
 \rho_1\!\left(\operatorname{PU}_{2}(\mathcal C_{2}(\mathbf F))\right),
 \rho_2\!\left(\mathcal C_{4}(\mathbf F)\right)
 \right].
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal C_2\) and \(\mathcal C_4\) are convolutions with strides of 2 and 4, \(\operatorname{PU}_r\) rearranges \(r\times r\) spatial blocks into the channel dimension, and \(\rho_0\) to \(\rho_2\) compress the channels of each path. \(\mathbf G\) enters a state-space module comprising local positional encoding, four-directional selective scanning, and a local feed-forward network \citep{gu2023mamba,liu2024vmamba}. Scanning propagates horizontally, vertically, and in the reverse directions to produce \(\mathbf O\), which is then upsampled and projected to obtain \(\mathbf S\) and \(\mathbf J\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oscd_context_flow}
 \mathbf O=\operatorname{SS2D}(\mathbf G),\qquad
 \mathbf S=\operatorname{Up}_{3}(\mathbf O),\qquad
 \mathbf J=\rho^r(\mathbf S).
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oscd_context_reconstruction}
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathbf R_3=\operatorname{Reconstruct}\!\big[&\mathbf S,\operatorname{Short}(\mathbf F),
 \operatorname{Global}_{3}(\mathbf F),\\
 &\mathbf E_3+\mathbf J,\mathbf E_4+\mathbf J,\mathbf E_5+\mathbf J\big].
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where \(\operatorname{Short}\) is a short convolutional branch, \(\operatorname{Global}_3\) performs global pooling, projection, and spatial-size restoration, and \(\operatorname{Reconstruct}\) applies convolutional reconstruction to the six inputs. \(\mathbf S\) retains the full output of the scanning branch, while \(\mathbf E_i+\mathbf J\) returns regional information to \(\mathbf D_3\) to \(\mathbf D_5\). The short convolutional and global branches supplement local responses and overall statistics, respectively. The regional reconstruction is denoted by \(\mathbf R_3\).

Local recovery progressively maps regional decisions onto finer grids. OSSD first upsamples \(\mathbf R_3\) to the \(\mathbf D_2\) grid and fuses it with projected \(\mathbf D_2\) to obtain \(\mathbf R_2\), then incorporates \(\mathbf D_1\) to reconstruct \(\mathbf R_1\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:oscd_reconstruction}
  \mathbf{R}_{2}
  =
  \mathcal{R}_{2}\!\left[
  \operatorname{Up}(\mathbf{R}_{3}),\rho_{2}^{d}(\mathbf{D}_{2})
  \right],
  \qquad
  \mathbf{R}_{1}
  =
  \mathcal{R}_{1}\!\left[
  \operatorname{Up}(\mathbf{R}_{2}),\rho_{1}^{d}(\mathbf{D}_{1})
  \right].
\end{equation}
where \(\rho_i^d\) is a detail projection and \(\mathcal R_i\) is the convolutional reconstruction mapping at the corresponding level. The classification head maps \(\mathbf R_1\) to binary classification outputs, which are restored to the input size by bilinear interpolation. Softmax and the selected threshold then produce the flood mask \(\hat{\mathbf M}\). The same softmax output provides the foreground probability \(p_f\) for each pixel.

During whole-scene inference, \(p_f\) is accumulated in overlapping areas using valid-pixel weights and used for mosaicking. The resulting probability variance and threshold-vote proportions identify candidate patches whose predictions are unstable across tiles. The filtered binary output and the original mosaic are exported separately to maintain consistency between predictions from different tiles.

\subsection{Change-Constrained Flood Depth Estimation}\label{sec:ccdepth}

The newly inundated extent obtained by change detection identifies locations where the water--land relationship has changed. Its boundary includes newly inundated margins, unchanged higher terrain, and locally missing observation pixels. Boundary-elevation-based depth methods generally extract water-level references from inundation boundaries and propagate them into the interior \citep{cohen2019floodwater,betterle2024water}. Change extents can be narrow and fragmented, with inconsistent boundary conditions, so a single boundary water level can readily produce inconsistent water-surface references within a connected component. CCDepth uses the change extent as the solution support and organizes boundary elevations into acceptable water-level intervals, jointly constraining water-surface estimation through the support domain, terrain, and boundary intervals.

The central formulation of CCDepth defines the water-surface solution space through boundary water-level intervals and interior terrain lower bounds, and reconstructs a continuous water surface within the support domain by solving a constrained energy using four-color coordinate descent. Inner- and outer-boundary samples jointly determine boundary water-level intervals that accommodate local fluctuations in boundary elevations. Terrain lower-bound constraints in the interior prevent the water surface from falling below the ground. A continuity term, expressed as a weighted sum of squared water-surface elevation differences between adjacent pixels, couples the water surface within each eight-connected component. These three constraints represent boundary water levels, ground references, and spatial continuity, respectively, so water-surface elevations within a component are jointly determined by boundary intervals, terrain lower bounds, and neighborhood continuity.

CCDepth takes the detection mask \(\hat{\mathbf M}\) and DEM \(Z\) as inputs and estimates depth within the newly inundated domain \(\Omega_f\) defined in Eq.~(\ref{eq:framework_support}). Depth is solved on the detection-mask grid, with terrain elevations read at the row and column positions corresponding to the mask. To distinguish boundary neighborhoods from interior constraints, \(\Omega_f\) is eroded with a \(3\times3\) window and the boundary band is extracted:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ccdepth_domains}
 \mathcal H_f=\operatorname{Erode}_{3\times3}(\Omega_f),\qquad
 \mathcal B_f=\Omega_f\setminus\mathcal H_f,
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal H_f\) is the interior obtained by \(3\times3\) erosion of the support domain, and \(\mathcal B_f\) is the boundary band within the support domain but outside this interior. Pixels in \(\mathcal H_f\) are subject to stronger terrain lower-bound constraints, whereas pixels in \(\mathcal B_f\) can adjust between the boundary water-level interval and the terrain lower bound. Let \(\mathcal C_p\) denote the eight-connected component containing pixel \(p\), and let \(\Omega_d=\Omega_Z\setminus\{p:\hat{\mathbf M}(p)=1\}\) denote the valid unchanged terrain domain.

Boundary water-level intervals are jointly constructed from inner-boundary samples in the boundary band and outer-boundary samples. For a boundary pixel \(p\), inner-boundary samples \(\mathcal W_p\) are extracted from pixels in the same component as \(p\) within the local window \(\mathcal N_{r_p}(p)\), while outer-boundary samples \(\mathcal D_p\) are extracted from unchanged areas adjacent to \(\mathcal C_p\):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ccdepth_samples}
 \mathcal W_p=\{q\in\mathcal N_{r_p}(p):q\in\mathcal C_p\},\qquad
 \mathcal D_p=\{q\in\mathcal N_{r_p}(p)\cap\Omega_d:\mathcal N_1(q)\cap\mathcal C_p\neq\emptyset\}.
\end{equation}
The median elevation, median absolute deviation (MAD), and dispersion scale are calculated separately for the samples on each side:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ccdepth_interval}
 \begin{aligned}
 m_w(p)&=\operatorname{median}_{q\in\mathcal W_p}Z(q),&
 m_d(p)&=\operatorname{median}_{q\in\mathcal D_p}Z(q),\\
 \sigma_w(p)&=\max\!\left[\sigma_0,\,1.4826\operatorname{MAD}_{q\in\mathcal W_p}Z(q)\right],&
 \sigma_d(p)&=\max\!\left[\sigma_0,\,1.4826\operatorname{MAD}_{q\in\mathcal D_p}Z(q)\right],\\
 L_p&=m_w(p)-\sigma_w(p),&
 U_p&=m_d(p)+\sigma_d(p),\qquad m_p=(L_p+U_p)/2.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where \(\sigma_0\) is the lower limit of dispersion, \(L_p\) and \(U_p\) define the water-level interval, and \(m_p\) is its midpoint. Inner-boundary samples provide terrain references within the inundated extent, while outer-boundary samples provide references from adjacent unchanged terrain. Together, they define an acceptable water-level range for the subsequent solution.

Initial boundary-sample weights jointly account for sample counts, slope, dispersion on both sides of the boundary, and elevation ordering. For a boundary pixel with complete samples, the initial weight is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ccdepth_weight}
 \beta_p^0=
 \min\!\left(1,\frac{\min(|\mathcal W_p|,|\mathcal D_p|)}{n_0}\right)
 \prod_{k=1}^{3}\frac{1}{1+\Delta_{p,k}^2},
\end{equation}
where \(n_0\) is the minimum sample count, \(\Delta_{p,1}=\theta_p/\theta_s\), \(\Delta_{p,2}=\sqrt{\sigma_w^2(p)+\sigma_d^2(p)}/\delta\), \(\Delta_{p,3}=\max(m_w(p)-m_d(p),0)/\sqrt{\sigma_w^2(p)+\sigma_d^2(p)}\), \(\theta_p\) is slope, \(\theta_s\) is the slope scale, and \(\delta\) is the dispersion scale. High-weight boundary candidates are further compared with the interval midpoints of peer candidates within the same component. When at least 3 high-weight candidates occur in the neighborhood, their midpoints and MAD are used to construct a consistency penalty:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ccdepth_peer}
 \beta_p=\beta_p^0\left\{1+\left[\frac{\max(0,|m_p-\tilde m_p|-3\tilde\sigma_p)}{\sigma_w(p)+\sigma_d(p)}\right]^2\right\}^{-1},
\end{equation}
where \(\tilde m_p\) and \(\tilde\sigma_p\) are the median and MAD of the interval midpoints of peer boundary candidates, respectively. This penalty retains samples consistent with neighboring boundary water levels and reduces the influence of local anomalous elevations on water-level intervals. A component enters the water-surface solver only if it contains at least 3 high-weight boundary candidates with a spatial span of at least 2 pixels. Components with insufficient support are assigned NoData.

Within an eligible component, let \(S_p\) be the unknown water-surface elevation and \(T_p=Z_p+d_{\min}\) be the terrain lower bound, where \(d_{\min}\) is the minimum positive depth. CCDepth constructs an energy from continuity, boundary intervals, terrain lower bounds, and boundary midpoints:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ccdepth_energy}
 \begin{aligned}
 J_C&=\sum_{(p,q)\in\mathcal E}w_{pq}(S_p-S_q)^2,\qquad w_{pq}=\lambda_C h_{\mathrm{ref}}^2/d_{pq}^2,\\
 J_B&=\sum_{p\in\mathcal B_f}\beta_p\operatorname{dist}(S_p,[L_p,U_p])^2,\qquad
 J_T=\sum_{p\in\mathcal B_f}\max(T_p-S_p,0)^2,\\
 J_M&=\sum_{p\in\mathcal B_f}\beta_p(S_p-m_p)^2,\qquad
 J_0=J_C+\lambda_B J_B+\lambda_T J_T,\qquad J_\mu=J_0+\mu J_M,
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal E\) is the set of eight-neighbor edges within the same component, \(w_{pq}\) is determined by the reference pixel scale \(h_{\mathrm{ref}}\) and pixel distance \(d_{pq}\), and \(\operatorname{dist}(S_p,[L_p,U_p])=\max(0,L_p-S_p,S_p-U_p)\). The parameters \(\lambda_C\), \(\lambda_B\), and \(\lambda_T\) control the relative contributions of continuity, boundary intervals, and terrain lower bounds, respectively, while \(\mu\) controls the strength of the boundary-midpoint term. Pixels in \(\mathcal H_f\) must also satisfy the hard constraint \(S_p\geq T_p\). To remove the influence of absolute scale, the objective energy is normalized by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ccdepth_normalized_energy}
 Q=\sum_{(p,q)\in\mathcal E}w_{pq}+\lambda_B\sum_{p\in\mathcal B_f}\beta_p+\lambda_T|\mathcal B_f|
\end{equation}
and subsequent convergence checks use \(E_0=J_0/Q\) and \(E_\mu=J_\mu/Q\). Normalization makes objective values comparable across components and pixel counts, and provides a common scale for convergence criteria for the primary and secondary objectives. This energy formulation jointly incorporates boundary water-level intervals, interior terrain lower bounds, and neighborhood continuity, allowing the water surface to vary continuously within each connected component while retaining flexibility where boundary constraints are inconsistent. The hard terrain lower bound in \(\mathcal H_f\) ensures that the water surface does not fall below the ground.

The water surface is initialized using a weighted combination of boundary water-level intervals. For an eligible component, the initial water level \(s_0\) is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ccdepth_initialization}
 s_0=\frac{\sum_{p\in\mathcal B_f}\beta_p m_p}{\sum_{p\in\mathcal B_f}\beta_p},\qquad
 S_p^{(0)}=
 \begin{cases}
  \max(s_0,T_p), & p\in\mathcal H_f,\\
  s_0, & p\in\mathcal B_f.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This initialization starts each connected component from a common water level, keeps the interior at or above the terrain lower bound, and leaves the boundary band free to adjust within the water-level intervals. Components with insufficient support do not enter the water-surface solver and are marked as NoData in the output.

Water-surface solution is organized by eight-connected components and uses four-color scanning. The continuity term couples water-surface elevations within each component. Pixels in each component are assigned to four update colors according to the parity of their row and column indices in the full image. Pixels of the same color are not eight-neighbors, and sequentially updating the four colors completes one sweep.

For pixel \(p\), a weighted water-surface elevation \(\bar S_p=\sum_{q\in\mathcal N_p}w_{pq}S_q/\sum_{q\in\mathcal N_p}w_{pq}\) is first calculated from its eight-neighborhood. Six candidate branches are then constructed according to the candidate's position relative to the boundary interval and terrain lower bound. Each branch includes only the applicable objective terms, giving the following closed-form update:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ccdepth_update}
\begin{aligned}
u_p^{(k)}&=\frac{\begin{aligned}
 &\sum_{q\in\mathcal N_p}w_{pq}S_q\\
 &+\chi_p\lambda_B\beta_p\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{r_k=\mathrm{low}\}}L_p+\mathbf{1}_{\{r_k=\mathrm{high}\}}U_p\right)\\
 &+\chi_p\lambda_T\mathbf{1}_{\{g_k=\mathrm{below}\}}T_p+\chi_p\mu\beta_pm_p
 \end{aligned}}{\begin{aligned}
 &d_p\\
 &+\chi_p\lambda_B\beta_p\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{r_k=\mathrm{low}\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{r_k=\mathrm{high}\}}\right)\\
 &+\chi_p\lambda_T\mathbf{1}_{\{g_k=\mathrm{below}\}}+\chi_p\mu\beta_p
 \end{aligned}},\\
s_p^{(k)}&=\Pi_{\mathcal F_p^{(k)}}\!\left(u_p^{(k)}\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where \(d_p=\sum_{q\in\mathcal N_p}w_{pq}\), \(\chi_p=\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal B_f}(p)\), \(r_k\) indicates whether the candidate water surface lies below, within, or above the boundary interval, and \(g_k\) indicates whether it lies below or above the terrain lower bound. The set \(\mathcal F_p^{(k)}\) is jointly defined by the corresponding interval and terrain positions; interior candidates must also satisfy the hard constraint \(S_p\geq T_p\). Thus, the boundary-interval term is active only in the branches below or above the interval, the soft terrain term in the boundary band is active only below \(T_p\), and the midpoint term is weighted by \(\beta_p\) in the boundary band. After each candidate is projected onto its feasible set, its local energy is calculated using Eq.~(\ref{eq:ccdepth_energy}), and the candidate with the lowest energy is selected to update \(S_p\). This pixelwise update incorporates boundary intervals, terrain lower bounds, and continuity into a single coordinate optimization process according to the branch conditions.

Each component is solved in two stages, using primary and secondary objectives. The primary stage solves \(E_0\) with \(\mu=0\). After convergence, secondary objectives \(E_\mu\) are attempted sequentially with \(\mu=\lambda_B[10^{-3},10^{-4},10^{-5},10^{-6}]\). A secondary solution is accepted if it converges at fixed \(\mu\) and the increase in the primary objective does not exceed \(\max(10^{-4}E_0^{\mathrm{base}},10^{-8})\). If no secondary solution passes, the component reverts to the primary solution. A component is assigned NoData if the primary objective fails to converge within the budget or violates hard constraints. Diagnostics are checked every 10 sweeps, including the fixed-point residual, relative changes in the normalized primary objective \(E_0\) and current total objective \(E_\mu\), total-objective monotonicity, hard-constraint violations, and diagnostic validity. A stage is considered converged when two consecutive checks satisfy the corresponding thresholds and feasibility conditions. The depth experiments use a fixed-point residual threshold of \(0.5\) m and an objective relative-change threshold of \(10^{-3}\), with at most 2000 sweeps for each primary or secondary objective.

Final depth is obtained as the difference between water-surface and terrain elevations in successfully solved components:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ccdepth_depth}
 \hat d_p=
 \begin{cases}
  \max(S_p-Z_p,0), & p\in\Omega_f\text{ and the component passes the audit},\\
  \text{NoData}, & \text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Successfully solved domains output both zero and positive depths, whereas locations with insufficient support or a failed primary objective are assigned NoData. Valid depths and NoData are marked separately in the output. Algorithm~\ref{alg:ccdepth} presents the main CCDepth solution procedure. Boundary-sample statistics, energy construction, and candidate updates are given in Eqs.~(\ref{eq:ccdepth_interval})--(\ref{eq:ccdepth_update}), and the main numerical settings are listed in Table~\ref{tab:method_config}.

\subsection{Loss Function}\label{sec:loss}\label{sec:training_objective}

HarmoSSM is optimized using focal and Tversky losses, with five auxiliary heads providing additional multiscale supervision. Focal loss \citep{lin2017focal} adjusts the classification loss at each labeled pixel:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:focal_loss}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{focal}}
  =
  -\frac{1}{N}
  \sum_{n=1}^{N}
  w_{y_n}\left(1-p_{n,y_n}\right)^{\gamma}
  \log p_{n,y_n},
  \qquad \gamma=2.
\end{equation}
where \(N\) is the number of labeled pixels, \(p_{n,y_n}\) is the predicted probability for label \(y_n\) at pixel \(n\), and \(\gamma=2\). The background and flood weights are \(w_0=0.25\) and \(w_1=0.75\), respectively. Tversky loss \citep{salehi2017tversky} uses the soft foreground counts \(\mathrm{TP}_b\), \(\mathrm{FP}_b\), and \(\mathrm{FN}_b\) to control missed detections and false alarms:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tversky_loss}
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tv}}=1-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}
 \frac{\mathrm{TP}_b+\epsilon_{\mathrm{Tv}}}{\mathrm{TP}_b+\alpha(e)\mathrm{FP}_b+\beta(e)\mathrm{FN}_b+\epsilon_{\mathrm{Tv}}},\qquad\alpha(e)=1-\beta(e).
\end{equation}
where \(B\) is the batch size, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{Tv}}=10^{-6}\), and \(e\) is the training epoch. The false-negative weight \(\beta(e)\) decreases linearly from 0.70 at epoch 1 to 0.55 at epoch 20 and remains constant thereafter. The main and auxiliary outputs use the same combination of focal and Tversky losses:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:segmentation_loss}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{seg}}
  =
  \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{focal}}^{\mathrm{main}}
  +
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tv}}^{\mathrm{main}}
  +
  \sum_{i=1}^{5}\omega_i(e)
  \left(
  \frac{1}{2}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{focal}}^{(i)}
  +
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tv}}^{(i)}
  \right).
\end{equation}
The relative weights of the five auxiliary heads are \((1,1,0.5,0.25,0.25)\). After normalization, they are multiplied by a common coefficient, which is 1 for the first 5 epochs, decreases linearly to 0.5 between epochs 5 and 20, and remains fixed thereafter.

To coordinate responses across scales, the stop-gradient quantity \(s=\max(p_{D1},p_{D2})\) serves as a support reference. The support-preservation term \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{sup}}\) is the mean of \(\max(s-p_{\mathrm{main}},0)\) over locations where \(s>0.60\). The coarse-scale consistency term \(\mathcal L_{\mathrm{coarse}}\) computes \(\max(p_j-s-0.15,0)\) where \(s<0.35\) and averages over \(j\in\{\mathrm{main},D4,D5\}\). The full training objective is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:total_loss}
  \mathcal{L}
  =
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{seg}}
  +
  \lambda_{\mathrm{sup}}(e)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}
  +
  \lambda_{\mathrm{coarse}}(e)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coarse}}.
\end{equation}
Both constraint weights are zero for the first 6 epochs, increase to 0.03 and 0.02, respectively, between epochs 6 and 15, and remain constant thereafter. The training objective optimizes HarmoSSM parameters. CCDepth independently performs deterministic solution under terrain and boundary constraints.

Without CQI, the change feature at level \(i\) is obtained by convolutional projection of the absolute difference, \(D_i=\Psi_i(|X_i^B-X_i^A|)\), where \(\Psi_i\) consists of a 1×1 convolution, batch normalization, and a 3×3 depthwise separable convolution. Without OSSD, the decoder uses progressive top-down reconstruction, sequentially fusing \(\mathbf D_5\) with \(\mathbf D_4\) through \(\mathbf D_1\). Each fusion consists of bilinear upsampling, channel concatenation, and a 3×3 convolution, followed at the end by the same classification head as the full model. The five auxiliary heads, combined focal and Tversky losses, support-preservation term, and coarse-scale consistency term are identical across configurations. Training data, optimizer, learning-rate schedule, number of training epochs, checkpoint- and threshold-selection rules, and whole-scene mosaicking are identical across configurations.

The depth-estimation comparison method is FwDET v2.0 \citep{cohen2019floodwater}. It assigns water-surface elevations within the flood extent from terrain elevations along its boundary and calculates water depth accordingly, providing a comparison with CCDepth within the category of boundary-elevation-constrained methods.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics and Protocols}\label{sec:experiment_metrics}

Flood is the positive class in flood change detection. \(TP\), \(FP\), \(FN\), and \(TN\) are confusion-matrix counts within the valid reference domain, and evaluation uses precision (P), recall (R), F1, IoU, and OA:
\begin{align}
 \mathrm{Precision}&=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}, & \mathrm{Recall}&=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\label{eq:precision}\\
 \mathrm{F1}&=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN}, & \mathrm{IoU}&=\frac{TP}{TP+FP+FN},\label{eq:f1}\\
 \mathrm{OA}&=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+FP+FN+TN}.\label{eq:oa}
\end{align}
Each scene undergoes tiled inference, overlap fusion, and whole-scene mosaicking before pixelwise statistics are calculated over the reference domain jointly defined by the imagery, labels, and valid masks. Each pixel is counted only once. All five metrics are reported as percentages. Results are reported separately for Zhengzhou, Zhuozhou, and Guangxi, with consistent reference extents and NoData rules across methods within each area.

Because inundated pixels occupy a small proportion of the reference domain, OA has limited ability to distinguish flood-identification performance. Methods are therefore ranked by IoU and F1, P and R describe the composition of errors, and OA is reported for reference.

Depth evaluation measures positive-depth coverage, errors over common-valid samples, and water-surface continuity. Let \(\mathcal P\) denote the candidate along-track reference points in Pakistan. The subset \(\Omega_r\) contains the 14,902 points with reference depths greater than 0.01 m. Positive-depth coverage is the proportion of these locations at which CCDepth or FwDET provides a valid positive depth:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:valid_depth_ratio}
 R_j=100\frac{|V_j|}{|\Omega_r|},
\end{equation}
where \(d_r\) is the along-track reference depth and \(V_j=\{p\in\Omega_r:d_j(p)>0.01\,\mathrm m,\ d_j(p)\text{ finite}\}\).

Depth errors are defined as reference minus estimated depth. Error statistics are based on 11,319 points valid for both methods. These are first grouped by native-grid row and column combinations, using the median reference depth within each group as the sample value to form 2,442 aggregated samples. Mean error, error standard deviation, mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean square error (RMSE) are then calculated over these aggregated samples:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:depth_error_stats}
 e_p=d_r(p)-d_j(p),\qquad
 \bar e=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}e_p,\qquad
 \mathrm{MAE}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\left|e_p\right|,\qquad
 \mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}e_p^{2}},
\end{equation}
where \(e_p\) is reference minus estimated depth, \(\bar e\) is mean error, \(\sigma_e\) is the error standard deviation, and \(N=2{,}442\) is the number of aggregated samples. The water-surface elevation gradient is calculated from elevation differences between adjacent valid solution pixels to describe water-surface spatial continuity.

For regional depth products, the statistical population is the total number of pixels within the HarmoSSM-detected extent. The valid-depth proportion is the percentage of this population with valid depth outputs and measures how fully the depth estimates cover the detected extent.

Local site evaluation uses the HarmoSSM-detected extent as the input for depth estimation. Estimates are represented by the median and minimum-to-maximum range of valid depths within the connected component containing each site. The median describes the center of the within-component depth distribution, while the range describes variation in submergence within the component. Reference intervals for photograph sites are interpreted from visible reference objects. Reference intervals for literature sites span the minimum to maximum of the available observations.

Site results are classified first by the position of the median relative to the reference interval. A median within the reference interval is classified as a median hit, including equality at either endpoint. If the median lies outside the interval but the estimated range intersects it, the result is classified as range overlap only, including contact at an endpoint. If the maximum estimated depth is below the lower reference bound, the result is classified as entirely below. All site results fall into these three categories. The first two categories jointly describe compatibility between the estimated and reference intervals.

The input-extent comparison evaluates how detection errors propagate into depth estimates. The manually delineated reference extent and the HarmoSSM-detected extent are each used as input to CCDepth, with the DEM, solver parameters, and depth-class thresholds fixed. Depths are grouped into six classes, 0 to 0.25~m, 0.25 to 0.5~m, 0.5 to 1~m, 1 to 2~m, 2 to 4~m, and greater than 4~m. For each method, transition matrices use pixels with valid depths under the reference-extent input as the reference domain. Matrices are normalized by row, and diagonal entries give depth-class agreement proportions. Columns also include Missed for detection omissions and No data for missing depth estimates within the detected extent. FwDET is evaluated under the same protocol.

For the shallowest class in Zhengzhou, FwDET's depth-class agreement proportion of 0.86 exceeds that of CCDepth. CCDepth has higher depth-class agreement in Zhengzhou and lower agreement in Zhuozhou, indicating that the effects of input-extent differences on depth classes vary among study areas.

For the deepest class, greater than 4~m, in Zhengzhou, depth-class agreement is 0.82 for CCDepth and 0.19 for FwDET. Transitions to the adjacent 2--4~m class account for 0.06 and 0.36, respectively, while No data proportions are 0.00 and 0.25. For the shallowest class in Zhuozhou, CCDepth's Missed and No data proportions are 0.14 and 0.06, compared with 0.26 and 0.18 for FwDET. For the deepest class in Guangxi, No data proportions are 0.04 and 0.09 for the two methods, respectively. These results distinguish three types of change: extent omissions, missing depth estimates, and depth-class transitions. They show that transferring the detected extent affects both output coverage and depth classification within retained areas.

Computational cost is assessed for both stages, using detection complexity and depth-estimation runtime. Figure~\ref{fig:framework_efficiency}(a) compares nine detection methods in Guangxi in terms of detection accuracy and computational complexity. Complexity records correspond to \(256\times256\) bi-temporal image tiles and include local model statistics and reference configurations from the literature. HarmoSSM requires approximately 175~G FLOPs, on the same order as CGNet and TTP, while its IoU exceeds theirs by 11.60 and 11.57 percentage points, respectively. TTP has the largest parameter count among the nine methods and an IoU of 70.22\%. These results show that HarmoSSM achieves higher detection accuracy at a computational cost similar to these methods.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Depth Constraints and Detection-Error Propagation under Shared Spatial Support}

DDFlood uses the intersection of the detected mask and the valid DEM as the CCDepth solution domain, so the detected extent determines both where depth is calculated and which boundary references are used. Depth results are therefore shaped at two levels. The constraint design of CCDepth determines how boundary elevations and terrain are translated into a water surface, and the spatial support jointly defined by the detected mask and DEM determines where errors enter and which boundary references are used. The Pakistan validation, which uses the GFM extent as input, mainly tests the former, whereas the site comparisons and input-extent comparison in the three study areas mainly test the latter.

The constraint ablation in Section~\ref{sec:ccdepth_evaluation} (Table~\ref{tab:ccdepth_ablation}) shows that the construction of boundary references substantially affects mean bias and dispersion. Using only inner-boundary samples increases the mean error to 0.5347~m, indicating that terrain inside the inundated extent is generally below the water level and introduces systematic underestimation when used alone. Using only outer-boundary samples increases the error standard deviation to 0.6874~m, reflecting local relief in unchanged terrain. The two-sided interval, with inner statistics defining the lower bound and outer statistics the upper bound, gives the lowest mean error and error standard deviation among all variants. Removing the terrain lower bound reduces positive-depth coverage to 86.12\%, indicating that the terrain lower bound helps maintain positive-depth coverage of interior pixels. Using a constant level per component increases the error standard deviation to 0.7431~m, showing that gradual water-surface variation within components reduces dispersion. Uniform boundary weights increase MAE to 0.5324~m. The peer-consistency penalty and the boundary-midpoint secondary objective have small effects on accuracy, with MAE values of 0.5002~m and 0.4921~m when removed. When the primary objective admits multiple solutions, the midpoint objective provides a basis for selecting a water-surface solution through a weak preference for interval midpoints, subject to a bounded increase in the primary objective.

Beyond the constraint mechanism, the cascade design of DDFlood also has practical deployment properties. The depth stage depends only on the detected extent and DEM and directly solves depths from the extent and terrain constraints. The two stages can be updated separately. With the DEM and solver parameters fixed, changes in depth classes can be attributed to differences in the input extent, so detection and depth-estimation errors can be assessed separately. The input-extent comparison in Section~\ref{sec:ddflood_framework_evaluation} relies on this property. CCDepth iterates only within components that satisfy the boundary-support conditions, so its computational cost varies with the size of the solved support domain, and its depth-estimation time is shorter than that of FwDET in all four evaluation areas (Fig.~\ref{fig:framework_efficiency}). These properties suit emergency settings where measured depths are scarce.

Detection errors enter the depth results through the solution domain and the boundary samples. Missed flood pixels and permanent-water pixels outside the detected extent contribute to outer-boundary statistics according to Eq.~\eqref{eq:ccdepth_samples} when they fall within the valid outer-boundary sampling band. Missed pixels are submerged, so their terrain lies below the flood water level. If DEM elevations over permanent water represent the water surface at acquisition and lie below the flood level, these samples may also provide low elevation references. Such samples may lower the upper bound of the interval and contribute to the overall underestimation. False alarms extend the solution domain onto higher terrain and alter connectivity. The input-extent comparison (Fig.~\ref{fig:two_stage_consistency}) shows that depth-class agreement corresponds weakly to detection IoU, and connectivity structure may influence error propagation. The IoU values of the three study areas differ by less than 4 percentage points, whereas the depth-class agreement proportions of CCDepth differ markedly. Zhuozhou, dominated by contiguous inundation, has the lowest agreement proportions, 0.66 to 0.71, possibly because extent errors along the margins of large components alter boundary samples and can affect component interiors through the continuity term. In Zhengzhou, dominated by scattered patches and narrow strips, extent errors may be more confined to individual small components, and the agreement proportions are 0.77 to 0.82. Guangxi has the highest IoU and agreement proportions of 0.74 to 0.78. CCDepth exceeds FwDET for all depth classes in Zhuozhou and Guangxi, and the two ranges overlap in Zhengzhou.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}

DDFlood targets regional-scale rapid flood assessment, and its depth products can serve as a reference for preliminary evaluation of submergence in affected areas. Two limitations remain in the current framework.

First, the scale mismatch between the detection mask and the DEM limits the precision of boundary references. CCDepth solves the water surface on the detection-mask grid with a pixel spacing of about 10~m, whereas FABDEM has a sampling interval of about 30~m and is mapped onto the mask grid by nearest-neighbor assignment, so the terrain information after mapping remains determined by the original DEM resolution. Inner- and outer-boundary samples within the boundary window therefore often come from the same or adjacent DEM cells, and narrow flooded strips lack independent interior terrain variation. Residual biases in FABDEM after building and forest removal also enter the boundary references. These factors may contribute to the positive mean error in the Pakistan validation and the underestimation at S5 to S7 in Zhuozhou.

Second, building obstruction can cause geometric and hydraulic connectivity to diverge. CCDepth assumes a continuous water surface within each connected component. Where the two types of connectivity diverge, the continuity constraint may merge water bodies that are actually separated, and the cascade design propagates this effect from detection to depth estimation. Zhengzhou is a densely built urban area, but the number of available sites is limited, so quantifying the magnitude of this effect requires additional site observations.

Future work can introduce higher-resolution terrain data to reduce the scale mismatch between the detection mask and the DEM, and incorporate flow-direction information and hydraulic-connectivity analysis to handle areas where geometric and hydraulic connectivity diverge. Training samples from additional sensors and polarizations, together with event-separated experiments, would further test the generalization of the detection stage. On this basis, joint optimization of detection and depth estimation can be explored to improve mapping consistency in complex settings.

\section{Conclusions}

This study develops DDFlood, a two-stage flood mapping framework that uses newly inundated extents detected by HarmoSSM as the solution support for CCDepth. HarmoSSM combines bi-temporal state and difference information through HA and CQI, and uses OSSD to reconstruct inundation extent from regional context and local detail. Its IoU values are 79.45\%, 77.83\%, and 81.79\% for the Zhengzhou, Zhuozhou, and Guangxi events, respectively, exceeding all eight comparison methods. In the ablation experiments, the full configuration improves IoU by 11.99--36.18 percentage points over the baseline, with the largest improvement in Zhengzhou, where scattered inundation predominates. CCDepth constructs water-level intervals from terrain statistics on both sides of the boundary and reconstructs the water surface under terrain lower-bound and continuity constraints. In the Pakistan validation, CCDepth achieves an MAE of 0.4910~m and an RMSE of 0.6917~m, reductions of 43.8\% and 35.2\% relative to FwDET, respectively, with positive-depth coverage of 92.36\%; estimated depths are generally underestimated. Constraint ablations show that two-sided boundary references, terrain lower bounds, and continuity constraints have different effects on depth errors and positive-depth coverage. When detected extents replace manually delineated reference extents, the proportions retaining the same depth class range from 0.66 to 0.82 across CCDepth's depth classes in the three study areas. Existing runtime records show that CCDepth requires less depth-estimation time than FwDET in all four evaluation areas.

The results show that using the detection mask to determine both the depth solution domain and boundary elevation references directly translates newly inundated extent identification into terrain-constrained depth estimates. Boundary elevation intervals, terrain lower bounds, and continuity constraints jointly yield lower depth-estimation errors and higher positive-depth coverage in this study's validation. The extent-replacement analysis suggests that detected boundaries and connectivity structure are important factors in the transfer from detection results to depth products, with boundary changes in contiguous inundation areas warranting particular attention. DDFlood maps floods using SAR imagery and public DEM data and is suited to rapid flood assessment where measured depth training samples are scarce. Future work will combine higher-resolution terrain, hydraulic-connectivity information, and independent depth validation in more regions to improve depth estimation in complex urban areas, and explore joint optimization of detection and depth estimation through differentiable water-surface solution.
